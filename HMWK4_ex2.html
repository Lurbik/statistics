HMWK 4 · Exercise 2

Sampling mean and sampling variance of penetration scores

Extension of HMWK 3: every penetration attempt now gets a score between 0 and 10 from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². Each of m hackers attacks n systems; for each hacker the sampling mean and variance of the n scores are computed online with Welford’s recurrences (Ex. 1) and then compared, across hackers, with μ and σ².

01Model and theoretical targets

Hacker j = 1, …, m attacks systems i = 1, …, n and obtains scores Xj,1, …, Xj,n, independent and identically distributed on [0, 10] with mean μ and variance σ². All hackers are identical by construction: any difference between them is pure sampling noise. For each hacker:

X̄j = (1/n) Σi Xj,i     Sj² = (1/(n − 1)) Σi (Xj,i − X̄j)²    (computed with Welford, one score at a time)

Across hackers: M̄ = (1/m) Σj X̄j and V̄ = (1/m) Σj Sj². Theory predicts

E[X̄] = μ,   Var(X̄) = σ²/n      E[S²] = σ²,   Var(S²) = (1/n)(μ₄ − σ⁴ (n − 3)/(n − 1))      E[SS/n] = σ² (n − 1)/n

μ₄ = E[(X − μ)⁴] is the fourth central moment. All theoretical values on this page are computed from the chosen distribution (by summation over its support, or analytically for the continuous uniform), never typed in.

07Conclusions

Run the simulation to see the computed comparison.

1 · What the comparisons show

For every n, including n = 2, the average of the sampling means stays close to μ and the average of the sampling variances stays close to σ²; the remaining gap is of the order of the Monte Carlo standard error and shrinks when m grows. This is the empirical face of unbiasedness: E[X̄] = μ and E[S²] = σ² for every n ≥ 2 (S² is not defined for n = 1). The divisor n − 1 is essential: the population-style SS/n is systematically too small by the factor (n − 1)/n, which is very visible at n = 2 (half of σ²) and negligible at n = 1000. The reason is that deviations are measured from X̄, which is itself fitted to the same data, so one degree of freedom is “used up”.

2 · Why this matters in inferential statistics

In practice we observe one sample — one hacker’s n scores — and want to say something about the unknown μ and σ² of the whole process. Unbiasedness means that X̄ and S² are not systematically off: repeated over many samples they are right on average. Together with Var(X̄) = σ²/n, it gives the standard error S/√n, which can be computed from that single sample; with the Central Limit Theorem, X̄ is approximately normal, which yields confidence intervals X̄ ± 1.96·S/√n and hypothesis tests about μ (section 05 checks how often such intervals actually contain μ). The m simulated hackers play the role of the “repeated samples” that inference reasons about but never observes.

3 · Effect of increasing n

Increasing the number of systems per hacker does not change the targets (both estimators stay unbiased), but it shrinks their spread: Var(X̄) = σ²/n decreases exactly like 1/n, while Var(S²) = (μ₄ − σ⁴(n − 3)/(n − 1))/n is not exactly proportional to 1/n but, for the distributions used here, behaves asymptotically like (μ₄ − σ⁴)/n; the corresponding standard deviations decrease like 1/√n. The estimators are consistent: one hacker with many systems gives values close to μ and σ². The distribution of X̄ also becomes more symmetric and Gaussian, while that of S² is right-skewed for small n and becomes approximately normal more slowly. In the penetration-testing reading: with few systems, identical hackers look very different (some seem “skilled”, some “unreliable”) purely by chance; only many attempts per hacker make their average score and their consistency reliable characteristics. Increasing m instead makes the averages M̄ and V̄ more precise (SE ∝ 1/√m) but does not reduce the variability of a single hacker’s estimates.
