01 What is being simulated?

Attacker j visits systems i = 1,…,n. Its increment Xj,i is drawn independently at every step; increments have the same distribution within each model. We additionally assume independence between attackers. Each of the m attackers is one sample trajectory of the same stochastic process.

Bernoulli(p)

Count successful steps

X = 1 with probability p, otherwise X = 0. A success adds one to the penetration score; a failure leaves it unchanged.

μ = p · σ² = p(1 − p) Sₙ ∼ Binomial(n, p)
Symmetric Rademacher

Measure a signed net score

X = +1 or −1 with equal probability 1/2. These represent a positive or negative score contribution. A negative score is not a negative count of penetrated systems.

μ = 0 · σ² = 1 Sₙ = 2Bₙ − n, Bₙ ∼ Binomial(n, 1/2)
n and m play different roles. Increasing n extends each path and changes the distribution of its endpoint. Increasing m provides more independent endpoints to estimate that distribution; it does not change the theoretical law of an individual path.
How to read this report
  1. The initial run simulates both models with the same seed. The Bernoulli and Rademacher buttons switch the view without generating new data.
  2. n is the number of systems visited by one attacker (the number of increments in one path); m is the number of attackers, i.e. independent replicas of the path.
  3. The endpoint selector compares checkpoints of the same simulated paths, not unrelated experiments.
  4. Each figure row shows the sum, the average and the sum divided by √n. Vertical axes adapt to the data, so read the tick values and the tables, not only the visual width.
  5. The Law of Large Numbers concerns Sₙ/n: it concentrates around p (Bernoulli) or 0 (Rademacher). Watch the fraction of attackers outside the band ±ε.
  6. The Central Limit Theorem concerns centered fluctuations. Rademacher is already centered with unit variance, so Sₙ/√n → N(0,1) in distribution; Bernoulli needs (Sₙ − np)/√[np(1−p)].
  7. Section 06 studies m automatically using nested prefixes of the same run (the first 50, 100, 200, … attackers): m changes the precision of the estimates and the aggregate (its mean grows with m for Bernoulli, only its spread grows for Rademacher), not the law of a single path.
  8. CSV export and print-to-PDF are available after a run.

06 Interpret n systems and m attackers

For the aggregate score Tₙ = Σj=1mSj,n, independence gives E[Tₙ] = mnμ and Var(Tₙ) = mnσ². The average over all attacker–system pairs, Tₙ/(mn), has variance σ²/(mn). A single observed aggregate does not itself provide an empirical variance for Tₙ; that would require repeating the whole m-attacker experiment.

In Bernoulli, this aggregate counts successful attacker–system pairs, not distinct compromised systems: several attackers can score on the same system. In Rademacher it is a signed aggregate with mean zero, whose standard deviation grows as √(mn). Real dependence between attacks, heterogeneous system difficulty, resource competition or changing p would violate the simple i.i.d. assumptions used here.

07 Why the limits differ

Write μ = E[Xᵢ] and σ² = Var(Xᵢ). Independence makes variances add. Dividing a random variable by a multiplies its variance by 1/a².

Law of Large Numbers: quantify concentration

For every fixed ε > 0: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) ≤ min(1, σ² / (nε²)) → 0.

This is Chebyshev's inequality applied to Sₙ/n. The simulation estimates the probability by the fraction of attackers outside the ±ε band. The exact finite-n probability is also computed from the binomial law. A finite simulation illustrates the theorem; the variance bound provides the argument for convergence.

Central Limit Theorem: keep fluctuations visible

For σ² > 0: Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ) / (σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution. Rademacher: Zₙ = Sₙ/√n. Bernoulli: Zₙ = (Sₙ − np) / √[np(1−p)], for 0 < p < 1.
The main distinction: Bernoulli Sₙ/√n has mean p√n and variance p(1−p). For any fixed p > 0 its mean drifts to +∞, so it does not approach N(0,1). At p = 0 or p = 1 the increments are deterministic and standardization divides by zero; the app marks that case as undefined. At finite n the scores are discrete, even when the histogram resembles a bell curve.

Convergence in distribution describes the cumulative distribution across many paths. It does not say that each individual Sₙ/√n trajectory settles to a normal random value. The Rademacher average concentrates at 0 while its √n-normalized total retains variance 1: there is no contradiction.

Simulation and numerical conventions

  • Each attacker uses a deterministic mulberry32 pseudorandom stream, seeded by a mixing function of the master seed, attacker index and model. This approximates independent draws for a teaching simulation; it is neither a cryptographic RNG nor a proof of statistical independence. The same seed/configuration reproduces the same endpoints.
  • All checkpoints are prefixes of the same paths. Results at different n are therefore related, not independent experiments. Increasing m keeps the first attackers unchanged; increasing the maximum n keeps earlier steps unchanged.
  • Empirical variances use the unbiased estimator with denominator m−1. The theoretical variances describe an individual attacker's score. Mean estimates have Monte Carlo standard error σ√(n/m) for totals, σ/√(nm) for averages and σ/√m for normalized totals.
  • The LLN table includes an approximate 95% Wilson interval for the estimated exceedance probability across independent attackers. Zero observed exceedances is not proof that the true probability is zero.
  • The normal CDF is evaluated with an approximation accurate to about 10⁻⁷. CDF distances include both sides of each jump. They are descriptive distances, not p-values or automatic hypothesis tests. A finite m introduces Monte Carlo error which can mask further improvement as n grows.
  • The exact finite-n PMF is evaluated by recurrence outward from its mode and normalized numerically. Extremely small tail terms may underflow to zero. A tolerance of 10⁻¹² × max(1,n) protects strict exceedance comparisons at floating-point boundaries.

Primary reading: MIT 18.05, Reading 6b: Central Limit Theorem and the Law of Large Numbers. The formulas above are applied explicitly to the two distributions in this assignment.
